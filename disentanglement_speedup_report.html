1 · The problem

One MLP weight took ~34 s per optimizer step in float64 with a matrix-exponential retraction. Across 72 MLP linears × ~100 steps that extrapolated to days of wall-clock — you couldn't iterate. We went down the stack to find where the time actually went.

3 · Is it still correct?

float32 and Cayley are not bit-identical to the original, so we measured the gap on real Qwen weights (20 steps). The disentanglement objective C (truncation cost = the Novice's reconstruction error) lands within ~10% at equal steps — and every step is ~50× cheaper, so a few extra steps close it. Orthogonality and the lossless round-trip confirm the transform itself is intact.

Cayley is an exact orthogonal map optimizing the identical objective f(Q); the ~10% is just a slightly different path to the same minimum. The ~2e-5 lossless error is pure float32 rounding — 4–5 orders below the energy truncation deliberately discards.

4 · Then we tried a GPU (RTX 3090)

The GPU story overturned two of my own guesses. Both are worth remembering.

Surprise 1 — matrix_exp's backward is the real cost
GPU (RTX 3090), n=3584. matrix_exp's forward is ~free, but its autograd backward (Fréchet derivative) costs ~460 ms — 12× Cayley's round-trip. This is why Cayley wins on GPU too.
Surprise 2 — GPU dense SVD is the bottleneck (and doesn't batch)
GPU SVD of 1792×2048. Dense cuSOLVER SVD is the bottleneck (and slower than the 158 ms CPU SVD!). Gram-eigh is 8× faster but approximates the small singular values — left out to keep the gradient exact.
Batched SVD [B,1792,2048] on GPU: per-matrix cost stays flat as B grows — cuSOLVER serializes the batch. Batching across layers gives ZERO speedup (the hypothesis that didn't survive contact with data).

8 · The output

from novice_io import load_disentangled
model = load_disentangled("novice_qwen_rf0.1.pt")
